\( \LaTeX \) symbols

This section is dedicated to amalgamate different \( \LaTeX \) symbol collection entries online, claiming to be representative, which the author has come across. Surely by no means is the following table exhaustive, yet it suffices when encountering and expressing many well-known mathematical facts, including the following sections.

Please feel free to search across the table using the search command in your browser; the symbols themselves are unable to be searched due to the limitation in MathJax.

Elementary binary operators

Symbol Command Name Mathematical Example Note
\( \pm \) \pm Plus or minus \( x^2 = 4 \implies x = \pm 2 \) Do not use to denote the result of a square root!
\( \mp \) \mp Minus or plus \( \cos{(\theta+\phi)} = \cos{\theta}\cos{\phi} \mp \sin{\theta}\sin{\phi} \) Ditto.
\( \times \) \times Cartesian product, or 'multiplication' in general in an elementary perspective. \( \mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N} = \{(0, 0), (0, 1), \ldots, (1, 0), (1, 1), \ldots, (2, 0), \ldots\} \) In Linear Algebra, it is used to denote a vector's cross-product.
\( \div \) \div Division \( 6 \div 3 = 2 \) Not commonly used in advanced mathematics; fractions or inverses are used instead.
\( \cdot \) \cdot Multiplication \( 2 \cdot 5 = 10 \) In Linear Algebra, it is used to denote a vector's dot-product.
\( \ast \) \ast Asterisk Let \( (H, \ast) \le (G, \ast) \) be a subgroup. Usually denotes another form of 'multiplication' in Abstract Algebra.
\( \star \) \star Star Let \( (G, \star) \) be a group. Ditto.
\( \dagger \) \dagger Dagger Usually as a symbol for footnote.
\( \ddagger \) \ddagger Double dagger Ditto.
\( \amalg \) \amalg Disjoint union of coproduct of sets \( A^\ast = \{1, 2\} \times \{1\},\ B^\ast = \{2, 3\} \times \{2\} \)
\( \implies A \amalg B = \{(1,1),(2,1),(2,2),(3,2)\} \)		 It has a larger counterpart called coproduct operator (cf. below).
\( \cap \) \cap Intersection of sets \( \{1,2,3\} \cap \{3,4,5\} = \{3\} \) It also conveys roughly the same idea outside Set Theory.
\( \cup \) \cup Union of sets \( \{1,2,3\} \cup \{3,4,5\} = \{1,2,3,4,5\} \) Ditto.
\( \uplus \) \uplus Union of multisets \( \{1,1,2\} \uplus \{3,4,4\} = \{1,1,2,3,4,4\} \) Used in Multiset Set Theory
\( \sqcap \) \sqcap Meet operation in lattices Define \( x \preceq y \) as \( x \mid y \).
\( 12 \sqcap 18 = \mathrm{gcd}(12,18) = 6 \)		 Seen in lattice theory.
\( \sqcup \) \sqcup Join operation in lattices Using the above definition,
\(4 \sqcup 6 = \mathrm{lcm}(4,6) = 12 \)		 Ditto.
\( \vee \) \vee Logical 'and' operation \( \top \vee \bot \equiv \bot, \top \vee \top \equiv \top \) Used primarily in formal logic.
\( \wedge \) \wedge Logical 'or' operation \( \bot \wedge \top \equiv \top, \bot \wedge \bot \equiv \bot \) Ditto.
\( \oplus \) \oplus (Many.) 'Symmetric difference' particularly in Set Theory In Set Theory, \( \{1,2,3\} \oplus \{3,4,5\} = \{1,2,4,5\} \). (The \( \bigtriangleup \) symbol is also used; cf. below.) Of course, it is used in many more disciplines. For example, in Linear Algebra, it denotes a direct sum.
\( \otimes \) \otimes Tensor product If \( \vec{v} = \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix} \) and \(\vec{w} = \begin{bmatrix}4 \\ 5 \\ 6\end{bmatrix \), then
\( \vec{v} \otimes \vec{w} = \begin{bmatrix}1 \times \begin{bmatrix}4 &s; 5 & 6\end{bmatrix} \\ 2 \times \begin{bmatrix}4 & 5 & 6\end{bmatrix}\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}4 & 5 & 6 \\ 8 & 10 & 12\end{bmatrix} \)		 Seen usually in Tensor Algebra.
\( \circ \) \circ Compound function \( f^{-1} \circ f = x, \quad f \circ f^{-1} = y \) This operator is right-associative, meaning, it reads from right to left.
\( \bullet \) \bullet Bullet Define \( (R, +, \bullet) \) to be a ring. Mainly as a custom symbol for multiplication-like custom operators.
\( \diamond \) \diamond Diamond Custom symbol for placeholder, custom operators or layout.
\( \lhd \) \lhd Left-hand arrowhead; proper normal subgroup \( H \lhd G \) Used mainly in Abstract Algebra.
\( \rhd \) \lhd Right-hand arrowhead \( G \rhd H \) As a reversed notation for the above.
\( \unlhd \) \unlhd Underlined left-hand arrowhead; normal subgroup \( H \unlhd G \) Used mainly in Abstract Algebra.
\( \unrhd \) \unrhd Underlined right-hand arrowhead \( G \unrhd H \) As a reversed notation for the above.
\( \oslash \) \oslash Hadamard division Given two matrices \( A, B \) with same dimention \( m \times n \),
if \( C = A \oslash B, C_{ij} = \frac{A_{ij}}{B_{ij}} \)		 Used as a custom operator otherwise.
\( \odot \) \odot Hadamard multiplication Similarly, if \( D = A \odot B, D_{ij} = A_{ij} \cdot B_{ij} \) Used as a custom operator otherwise.
\( \bigcirc \) \bigcirc Big circle Custom symbol for placeholder, custom operators or layout.
\( \triangleleft \) \triangleleft Left triangle Custom symbol for placeholder, custom operators or layout.
\( \Diamond \) \Diamond Big diamond Custom symbol for placeholder, custom operators or layout.
\( \bigtriangleup \) \bigtriangleup Big triangle up; symmetric difference In Set Theory, \( \{1,2,3\} \bigtriangleup \{3,4,5\} = \{1,2,4,5\} \) Custom symbol for placeholder, custom operators or layout.
\( \bigtriangledown \) \bigtriangledown Big triangle down Custom symbol for placeholder, custom operators or layout.
\( \Box \) \Box Box Custom symbol for placeholder, custom operators or layout.
\( \triangleright \) \triangleright Right triangle Custom symbol for placeholder, custom operators or layout.
\( \triangledown \) \triangledown Down triangle Custom symbol for placeholder, custom operators or layout.
\( \setminus \) \setminus Set minus \( \{1,2,3,4,5\} \setminus \{2,4\} = \{1,3,5\} \) Used almost exclusively in set theory.
\( \wr \) \wr Wreath product \( \lvert A \wr_\Omega H \rvert = \lvert A \rvert^{\lvert \Omega \rvert} \lvert H \rvert \) Used mainly in Group Theory, in Abstract Algebra.
\( \sqrt[n]{x} \) \sqrt[n]{x} or \sqrt{x} Root symbol \( \sqrt{4} = 2, \sqrt[3]{27} = 3, \sqrt[n]{x} = x^{\frac1n} \) Omit the \( n \) in the bracket if only the square root sign is desired.
\( a^b \) a^b Exponent \( 3^3 = 27, 4^4 = 256 \) This should not be considered a 'binary operator' if strictly in \( \LaTeX \)-speak, yet it behaves similarly, so the author has put it down here.

Note: operators that are extremely common or useful, especially those directly available on the usual keyboards, can be directly keyed into the formulae. Examples include plus (+), minus (-) and colon (:, as proportion).

Relation Symbols

Symbol Command Name Mathematical Example Note
\( \le \) \le Less than or equal to \( 3 \le 5, 4 \le 4 \) As the stimulus and the foundational notion of an 'order' in advanced mathematics.
\( \ge \) \ge Greater or equal to \( 5 \ge 3, 4 \ge 4 \) Ditto.
\( \neq \) \neq Not equal to \( 3 \neq 4 \) This is one of the most taught notions in basic mathematics.
\( \sim \) \sim Similar to Define relation \( x \sim y \) if \( x \mid y \). Apart from the above stimulus, this symbol is also used in many disciplines. For example, in Geometry, it means 'similar shapes'.
\( \simeq \) \simeq Similar or equal to; Having the same cardinality of In set theory, \( \mathbb{N} \simeq \mathbb{Z} \simeq \mathbb{Q} \) Stripped set theory away, it also means 'asymptotically equivalent to' or generically 'approximately equal to', despite a dedicated symbol for the latter.
\( \ll \) \ll Far less than \( \pi \ll e^{e^e} \) The usage is contingent on context and is rather subjective.
\( \gg \) \gg Far greater than \( 10^50 \gg e \) Ditto.
\( \doteq \) \doteq Approximately equal to (in Calculus etc.) When \( x \rightarrow 0\), \( \frac{\sin{x}}{x} \doteq 1 \) This is a variation of the 'approximately equal to' operator.
\( \subset \) \subset Proper subset of \( \mathbb{N} \subset \mathbb{R} \) Generally used in set theory.
\( \supset \) \supset Proper superset of \( \mathbb{Q} \supset \mathbb{N} \) Ditto.
\( \approx \) \approx Approximately equal to \( \pi \approx e \approx \sqrt{g} \approx 3 \) This is the most commonly known symbol dedicatd to denoting 'approximately equal to', yet sometimes it may be subjective.
\( \asymp \) \asymp Asymptotic operator; Alternative for the big-\( \Theta \) \( f \asymp g \iff \exists C,D > 0: C\lvert g \rvert \le \lvert f \rvert \le D \lvert g \rvert \) This is considered part of the Asymptotic Notation.
\( \subseteq \) \subseteq Subset of \( \mathbb{N} \subseteq \mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Q} \subseteq \mathbb{Q} \) This is used the most in Set Theory.
\( \supseteq \) \supseteq Superset of \( \mathbb{Q} \supseteq \mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{R} \supseteq \mathbb{R} \) As a reverse of the above symbol.
\( \cong \) \cong Congruent to \( \triangle ABC \cong \triangle DEF \) Used mainly in elementary Geometry.
\( \smile \) \smile Cup product In Algebraic Topology, if \( \alpha^p, \beta^q\) are a \( p \)- and \( q \)-cochain respectively, then
\( (\alpha^p \smile \beta^q)(\sigma) = \alpha^p(\sigma \circ \iota_{0,1,\ldots,p}) \cdot \beta^q(\sigma \circ \iota_{p,p+1,\ldots,p+q}) \)		 Also used similarly in Homological Algebra.
\( \frown \) \frown Cap product In Algebraic Topology, a singular chain \( \sigma: \Delta^p \rightarrow X \) with singular cochain \( \psi \in C^q(X; R) \) has its cap product
\( \sigma \frown \psi = \psi(\sigma \mid_{[v_0, \ldots, v_q]}) \sigma \mid_{[v_q, \ldots, v_p]} \)		 Ditto.
\( \sqsubset \) \sqsubset Square proper subset In Multiset Theory,
\( \{a,a,b\} \sqsubset \{a,a,b,b\} \)		 Also used in many other disciplines, such as Computer Science, the usual Set Theory and Lattice Theory.
\( \sqsupset \) \sqsupset Square proper superset \( \{a,a,b,b\} \sqsupset \{a,a,b\} \) Ditto.
\( \sqsubseteq \) \sqsubseteq Square subset of \( \{a,a,b\} \sqsubseteq \{a,a,b\} \) Ditto.
\( \sqsupseteq \) \sqsupseteq Square superset of \( \{a,a,b\} \sqsupset \{a,a,b\} \) Ditto.
\( \equiv \) \equiv Equivalent to; Congruent to In Number Theory, we have \( a^{\varphi{n}} \equiv 1 \pmod{p} \). Also widely used in logic; used in discussing 'identities' in elementary mathematics.
\( \propto \) \propto Proportional to \( PV \propto nT \) When denoting inverse proportion, apply reciprocal to the right-hand-side.
\( \bowtie \) \bowtie Bow tie; natural join operator in Relational Algebra In Relational Algebra, \( R \bowtie S = \{r \cup S \mid r \in R \wedge s \in S \wedge Fun(r \cup S)\} \)
\( \in \) \in In \( 3 \in \mathbb{Z} \)
\( \ni \) \ni Contains \( \mathbb{Z} \ni 3 \)
\( \prec \) \prec Preceding Define \( x \prec y \) if \( x \mid y \). Used mainly in advanced Algebra to avoid confusion with \( \le \).
\( \succ \) \succ Succeeding In the above definition, \( y \succ x \). Ditto.
\( \vdash \) \vdash Entails, derives \( \Gamma \vdash \phi \) Used in formal logic; also called a 'turnstile'.
\( \dashv \) \dashv Is sufficient for \( \phi \dashv \Gamma \) Ditto.
\( \perp \) \perp Perpendicular to \( \overline{AB} \perp \overline{CD} \) This notion is mainly used in Geometry.
\( \parallel \) \parallel Parallel to \( \overline{RS} \parallel \overline{XY} \) Ditto.
\( \mid \) \mid Divides \( 3 \mid 15 \) This notion is mainly used in Number Theory.

Formulae and important consequences

(Elementary) Number Theory

(Note: in the following table, \( a, b, c, k \) are all integers and \( p \) is a prime, unless otherwise specified.)

Mathematical representation Example Note
\( a \mid b \implies a \mid bc \) \( 2 \mid 14 \) so \( 2 \mid 182 \)
\( a \mid b \wedge b \mid a \implies a = \pm b \) \( a \) could only be either 3 or -3 if \( a \mid 3 \) and \( 3 \mid a \)
\( c \ne 0 \Longrightarrow a \mid b \iff ac \mid bc \) It makes sense when \( 3 \mid 18 \) when \( 54 \mid 324 \) and vice versa
\( a \mid b \wedge b \mid c \implies a \mid c \) Because \( 3 \mid 15 \) and \( 15 \mid 75 \), then clearly \( 3 \mid 75 \) Known as the transitivity property of divisibility
Any \( a \) can be written as \( bk + r \) where \( r \) is an integer such that \( 0 \le r < b \) \( 92 = 7 \cdot 13 + 1 \) and \( 126 = 7 \cdot 18 + 0 \) Known as the Division Algorithm
If \( p \mid ab \) then either \( p \mid a \) or \( p \mid b \) (assume \( a \ne b \)) Note that \( 7 \mid 91 = 7 \cdot 13 \) and \( 7 \mid 7 \) but \( 7 \nmid 13 \) Known as the Euclid's Lemma
\( \mathrm{gcd}(ac, bc) = c\mathrm{gcd}(a, b) \) and \( \mathrm{lcm}(ac, bc) = c\mathrm{lcm}(a, b) \) \( \mathrm{gcd}(30, 42) = 6 \) whilst \( 2 \cdot \mathrm{gcd}(30/2=15, 42/2=21) = 3 \); the rest follows
\( \mathrm{gcd}(a, b) \cdot \mathrm{lcm}(a, b) = ab \) Look at \( \mathrm{gcd}(35, 75) = 5 \) and \( \mathrm{lcm}(35, 75) = 525 \). Then \( 5 \cdot 525 = 2\ 625 = 35 \cdot 75 \)
If \( a = bk + r \) where \( r \) satisfies the constraints in the Division Algorithm, then \( \mathrm{gcd}(a, b) = \mathrm{gcd}(b, r) \) \( 144 = 32 \cdot 4 + 16 \) whilst \( \mathrm{gcd}(144, 32) = 16 \) and \( \mathrm{gcd}(32, 16) = 16 \) This is extremely useful in proving the Euclidean Algorithm
\( \exists \) pair of integral solution \( (\alpha, \beta) \) to a Diophantine equation \( ax + by = c \iff \mathrm{gcd}(a, b) \mid c \) It is immediately apparent that for \( 93x + 33y = 12 \) there is solution \( (5, -14) \), whilst no matter how would one not be able to find integral solution to \( 15x + 35y = 2 \) This is very useful 'guiding lamp' in finding integral solution when solving Diophantine equations
Every positive integer excluding 1 can be written uniquely as product of prime factors (a sole factor is acceptable: a prime has only one prime factor) Thanks to the commutativity property, no matter how one factorises, \( 1\ 001 \) is always equal to \( 7 \cdot 11 \cdot 13 \) regardless of order This is the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic
The integral congruence modulo \( n \) is an equivalent relation Omitted This includes reflexivity, symmetry and transitivity
If any single pair of any two numbers \( n_a, n_b \) from the set comprising purely integers \( \{n_1, n_2, \ldots, n_k\} \) gives \( \mathrm{gcd}(n_a, n_b) \), and \( \{r_1, r_2, \ldots, r_k \} \) are any numbers, then \( \exists x \) satisfying \[ x \equiv r_1 \pmod n_1 \] \[ x \equiv r_2 \pmod n_2 \] \[ \vdots \] \[ x \equiv r_k \pmod n_k \] Omitted This states the famous Chinese Remainder Theorem (first discussed in Sun-tzu Suan-ching)
If \( a \not\equiv 0 \pmod p \) then \[ a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p \] \( 10^{126} \equiv 1 \pmod{127} \) This is the Fermat's (Little) Theorem
More generally, if \( \mathrm{gcd}(a, n) = 1 \) then \[ a^{\phi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod n \] \( 10^{192} \equiv 1 \pmod{221} \) This is the Euclid's (Totient) Theorem
The value of \( \phi(n_1n_2) \), where \( n_1, n_2 \in \mathbb{Z}, \quad \mathrm{gcd}(n_1, n_2) = 1 \) is \( \phi(n_1)\phi(n_2) \) \( \phi(221) = \phi(17)\phi(13) = 16 \cdot 12 = 192 \) This is one of the available methods to compute Euler's totient function; see below
Assume that the factorisation of a number \( a \) is \( p_1^{\alpha_1} p_2^{\alpha_2} \ldots p_k^{\alpha_k} \), or more formally, \[ a = \prod_{i=1}^k p_i^{\alpha_i}\text{,} \] \[ \phi(a) = a \prod_{i=1}^k \left(1 - \dfrac{1}{p_i}\right) = a \left(1 - \dfrac{1}{p_1}\right)\left(1 - \dfrac{1}{p_2}\right) \cdots \left(1 - \dfrac{1}{p_k}\right) \] The value of \( \phi(221) \) above can be also calculated in this way; 192 should be obtained This is a general way to calculate the value of Euler's totient function
If \( (n-1)! \equiv -1 \pmod n \) holds, then \( n \) is a prime; vice versa \( 4! = 24 \equiv 4 \pmod 5 \) This is the Wilson's Theorem. Although it guarantees a converse, it is impractical when \( n \) is large
If \( \mathrm{gcd}(a, n) = 1 \), the congruence \[ ax + b \equiv 0 \pmod n \] has a solution \[ x \equiv -a^{\phi(n)-1}b pmod n \] Omitted This is a direct consequence of Euler's theorem

Calculus

(Note: in the following, \( a, b, c, n \) denote real, constant numbers and \( x, y \) are variables, unless otherwise specified)

  • \[ \dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x} a = 0 \]
  • \[ \dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x} x^n = nx^{n-1} \]
  • \[ \dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x} \log_a x = \dfrac{1}{x \ln a} \]
  • \[ \dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x} \ln x = \dfrac{1}{x} \]
  • \[ \dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x} a^x = a^x \cdot \ln a \]
  • \[ \dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x} e^x = e^x \]
  • \[ \dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x} \sin x = \cos x \]
  • \[ \dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x} \cos x = -\sin x \]
  • \[ \dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x} \tan x = \sec^2 x \]
  • \[ \dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x} \cot x = -\csc^2 x\]
  • \[ \dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x} \sec x = \sec x \tan x \]
  • \[ \dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x} \csc x = -\csc x \cot x \]
  • \[ \dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x} \arcsin x = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}} \]
  • \[ \dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x} \arccos x = -\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}} \]
  • \[ \dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x} \arctan x = \dfrac{1}{1+x^2} \]
  • \[ \dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x} \mathrm{arccot} x = -\dfrac{1}{1+x^2} \]
  • \[ \dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x} \mathrm{arcsec} x = \dfrac{1}{x\sqrt{x^2-1}} \]
  • \[ \dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x} \mathrm{arccsc} x = \dfrac{1}{x\sqrt{x^2-1}} \]
  • \[ \int \dfrac{1}{a + bx} \mathrm{d}x = \dfrac1b \ln \lvert a + bx \rvert + c \]
  • \[ \int (a + bx)^n \mathrm{d}x = \dfrac{(a + bx)^{n+1}}{b(n+1)} + c \quad (n \ne -1) \]
  • \[ \int \dfrac{x}{(a + bx)^2} \mathrm{d}x = \dfrac{1}{b^2} \left(\dfrac{a}{a+bx} + \ln \lvert a + bx \rvert \right) + c \]
  • \[ \int \dfrac{x^2}{(a + bx)^2} \mathrm{d}x = \dfrac{1}{b^3} \left( a + bx - \dfrac{a^2}{a + bx} - 2a \ln \lvert a + bx \rvert \right) + c \]
  • \[ \int \dfrac{1}{x(a + bx)} \mathrm{d}x = -\dfrac1a \ln \left\lvert \dfrac{a+bx}{x} \right\rvert + c \]
  • \[ \int \dfrac{1}{(x+a)(x+b)} \mathrm{d}x = \dfrac{1}{b-a} \ln \left\lvert \dfrac{x+a}{x+b} \right\rvert + c \]
  • \[ \int \dfrac{1}{x^2(a+bx)^2} \mathrm{d}x = -\dfrac{1}{ax} + \dfrac{b}{a^2} \ln \left\lvert \dfrac{a+bx}{x} \right\rvert + c \]
  • \[ \int \dfrac{1}{x(a+bx)^2} \mathrm{d}x = \dfrac{1}{a(a+bx)} - \dfrac{1}{a^2} \ln \left\lvert \dfrac{a+bx}{x} \right\rvert + c \]
  • \[ \int \dfrac{1}{x^2(a+bx)^2} \mathrm{d}x = -\dfrac{1}{a^3} \left( \dfrac{a+bx}{x} - 2b \ln \left\lvert \dfrac{a + bx}{x} \right\rvert - \dfrac{b^2x}{a+bx} \right) + c \]
  • \[ \int \dfrac{1}{a + bx^2} \mathrm{d}x = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{ab}} \arctan \dfrac{x\sqrt{ab}}{a} + c \qquad \text{ for $a, b$ with same signs} \]
  • \[ \int \dfrac{1}{a + bx^2} \mathrm{d}x = \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{-ab}} \ln \left\lvert \dfrac{a+\sqrt{-abx}}{a-\sqrt{-abx}} \right\rvert + c \qquad \text{ for $a, b$ with different signs} \]
  • \[ \int \dfrac{x}{a + bx^2} \mathrm{d}x = \dfrac{1}{2b} \ln \lvert a + bx^2 \rvert + c \]
  • \[ \int \dfrac{x}{a^2 \pm b^2x^2} \mathrm{d}x = \dfrac{1}{\pm 2b^2} \ln \lvert a^2 \pm b^2x^2 \rvert + c \]
  • \[ \int \dfrac{1}{a^2 + b^2x^2} \mathrm{d}x = \dfrac{1}{ab} \arctan \dfrac{bx}{a} + c \]
  • \[ \int \dfrac{1}{a^2 - b^2x^2} \mathrm{d}x = \dfrac{1}{2ab} \ln \left\lvert \dfrac{a+bx}{a-bx} \right\rvert + c \]
  • \[ \int \dfrac{1}{x(a^2 \pm b^2x^2)} \mathrm{d}x = \dfrac{1}{2a^2} \ln \left\lvert \dfrac{x^2}{a^2 \pm b^2x^2} \right\rvert + c \]
  • \[ \int \dfrac{1}{x^2(a^2 + b^2x^2)} \mathrm{d}x = -\dfrac{1}{a^2x} - \dfrac{b}{a^3} \arctan \dfrac{b}{a}x + c \]
  • \[ \int \dfrac{1}{(a^2+b^2x^2)^2} \mathrm{d}x = \dfrac{x}{2a^2(a^2+b^2x^2)} + \dfrac{2a^3b} \arctan \dfrac{bx}{a} + c \]
